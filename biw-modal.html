Simplified Body-in-White beam-frame model · Pure Python · 56 nodes · 110 beam elements

Why BIW Modal Analysis Matters

The Body-in-White (BIW) is the bare structural skeleton of a vehicle — everything before doors, glass, engine, and trim are added. Understanding its dynamic behavior is critical for two reasons: structural safety and ride comfort.

🔧 Structural Safety

  • Crash performance depends on how the BIW deforms and absorbs energy — the same structure that vibrates in modal analysis carries crash loads
  • Fatigue life — resonant vibrations amplify cyclic stresses; if a road excitation frequency matches a mode, components fail much sooner
  • Global stiffness — the first torsion and bending frequencies directly indicate overall structural stiffness (target: torsion >30 Hz, bending >40 Hz)

🎵 NVH & Ride Comfort

  • Noise, Vibration & Harshness (NVH) — passengers feel structure-borne vibration from engine, road, and wind; BIW modes below ~50 Hz are directly perceptible
  • Boom & drone — if a BIW mode frequency coincides with engine firing order, the cabin amplifies low-frequency booming noise
  • Steering feel — an insufficiently stiff front-end causes "cowl shake" that degrades steering precision and driver confidence
  • Door closing quality — higher global stiffness produces the satisfying "thunk" rather than a tinny rattle

📊 Industry Benchmarks (typical sedan)

  • 1st Torsion mode: 25–35 Hz
  • 1st Lateral bending: 35–45 Hz
  • 1st Vertical bending: 40–55 Hz
  • Total BIW mass (bare): 250–400 kg

Free-free modal analysis means the structure is unconstrained — no supports, no boundary conditions. This isolates the BIW's intrinsic dynamic properties from any mounting effects, making results directly comparable across different vehicle designs.

The Simplified BIW Model

Isometric view of the beam-frame BIW model

This is a 3D Euler-Bernoulli beam-frame model representing a mid-size sedan's BIW. Each structural member (pillar, rail, cross-member) is modeled as a beam element with 6 DOF per node (3 translations + 3 rotations), giving 12 DOF per element.

Method & Theory

Euler-Bernoulli Beam Element

Each beam element has 12 degrees of freedom (6 per node): 3 translations (u, v, w) and 3 rotations (θx, θy, θz). The element stiffness matrix combines axial, bending (two planes), and torsional stiffness. A consistent mass matrix is used for accurate dynamic behavior.

Coordinate Transformation

Since beams have arbitrary 3D orientation, each element's local stiffness and mass matrices are transformed to global coordinates via a 12×12 rotation matrix derived from the element's direction cosines.

Eigenvalue Problem

K · φ = ω² · M · φ

Solved using scipy.linalg.eigh(K, M) — a generalized symmetric eigenvalue solver.
The first 6 modes (ω ≈ 0) are rigid-body modes and are filtered out.
